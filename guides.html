How to find your size when shopping online

Size labels vary between brands, so the number on your favourite dress is only a starting point. Measuring yourself takes five minutes and saves most returns.

  1. Measure, don’t guess. Use a soft tape over light clothing. Bust: the fullest point, tape level across your back. Waist: the narrowest point, usually just above the navel. Hips: the fullest point around your seat.
  2. Compare with the retailer’s own chart. Every shop publishes its own size chart. If you fall between two sizes, size up for fitted or non-stretch fabrics and size down for stretchy knits.
  3. Read the fabric line. Cotton and linen have little give; blends with 2–5% elastane stretch and recover. “Relaxed” or “oversized” cuts are designed to be roomy.
  4. Check the model’s size. Many product pages say what size the model wears and how tall she is. It’s a quick way to judge length.
  5. Know the return window first. Look at the return period, who pays return shipping, and whether sale items can be returned before you check out.

Choosing a hair styling tool

The right tool depends on your hair type and the finish you want. A few specs matter more than the marketing.

  • Curling wand vs. iron: wands (no clamp) give looser, beachier waves; irons with a clamp give more defined, uniform curls. Barrel size sets curl size: 25 mm for tighter curls, 32–38 mm for soft waves.
  • Air stylers and hot-air brushes dry and style at the same time with lower heat than plates or barrels, which suits fine or fragile hair and fast routines.
  • Adjustable temperature is the most useful feature. Fine or colour-treated hair generally needs lower settings; thick or coarse hair can take more.
  • Barrel and plate material: ceramic heats evenly, tourmaline is often added to reduce frizz, and titanium heats very quickly (best for experienced users).
  • Always use a heat protectant and let hair dry fully before using hot tools.

Buying shoes online that actually fit

Fit is everything with shoes, and online you can’t try before you buy. These checks help.

  • Measure your foot length in centimetres at the end of the day, when feet are slightly larger, and compare it with the retailer’s chart rather than your usual size.
  • Width matters as much as length. Pointed toes and narrow lasts can feel a size smaller. Look for “wide fit” if you often feel pressure at the sides.
  • Heel height and comfort: block heels and platforms spread weight more evenly than stilettos. For long days, heels under about 7 cm (2.75 in) are easier to wear.
  • Read the materials. Leather and suede stretch slightly with wear; synthetics and patent finishes mostly don’t.
  • Try new shoes indoors first on a clean floor so you can still return them if they don’t fit.

A simple skincare routine that works

You don’t need ten steps. A consistent, simple routine is easier to keep and easier to judge.

  1. Cleanse with a gentle cleanser morning and night. If your skin feels tight afterwards, the cleanser may be too harsh.
  2. Treat with one active ingredient at a time, such as vitamin C in the morning or a retinoid at night, so you can tell what’s working.
  3. Moisturise to support your skin barrier, even if your skin is oily (choose a lightweight gel).
  4. Protect with a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher every morning. This is the step that makes the biggest long-term difference.

Patch test new products on a small area for a few days first. This guide is general information, not medical advice. See a dermatologist for persistent skin concerns.

Body jewelry basics: materials and sizes

For piercings, especially new ones, material and size matter more than style.

  • Implant-grade titanium (ASTM F136) is lightweight, nickel-free and widely recommended for initial piercings and sensitive skin.
  • Surgical steel is durable and affordable but contains a small amount of nickel, so it may not suit nickel-sensitive skin.
  • Solid 14k or 18k gold (not plated) is suitable for healed piercings. Plating can wear through over time.
  • Gauge is the thickness of the bar. Most ear piercings are 18g or 16g; noses are often 20g or 18g. Higher numbers mean thinner jewelry.
  • Length or diameter should leave a little room for swelling in new piercings. A piercer can measure you for a downsize once it heals.

Wigs and extensions: what the terms mean

Wig listings are full of jargon. Here’s what the common terms mean so you can compare like for like.

  • Human hair vs. synthetic: human hair can be heat-styled and coloured and lasts longer with care; synthetic is lower-cost and holds its style but usually can’t take high heat.
  • Lace front, 13×4, 5×5 and HD lace: the numbers are the lace area in inches (more lace means more parting space). HD lace is thinner and blends more easily with skin.
  • Glueless wigs use combs, bands or adjustable straps instead of adhesive, which makes them quicker to put on and take off.
  • Density (for example 150% or 180%) is how full the wig looks. Higher density means more volume.
  • Length is usually measured straight. Curly and wavy textures look shorter once worn.
  • Care: wash gently with sulfate-free products, let it air dry on a stand, and use a heat protectant before styling human hair.
